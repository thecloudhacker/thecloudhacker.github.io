French Fridge Fiddling

In a targeted attack on Internet-connected fridges, major supermarkets Carrefour, Coopérative U, Intermarché, Monoprix, among others, were hit by the cyber attack which left the devices heating and spoling the produce.

The affected equipment includes cold-storage units that hold food before reaching supermarket shelves. The criminals behind the attack raised temperatures to unsafe levels, spoiling food and in some cases starting fires. Some affected retailers used refrigeration controllers produced by Danish company Danfoss.

The team at Claroty detailed a vulnerability in August 2025. It has not been confirmed yet as to the entry point of this incident.

Industrial systems have long been an avenue of attack on large process plants, but this attack proves how vulnerable the food supply chain is.

Shodan searches for Internet-facing Danfoss controller interfaces indicated more than 4,500 endpoints were exposed as of Monday, with more than half of those located in France.

Whilst these types of Internet-connected devices support centralised management by maintenance teams, they must be kept patched and up-to-date with significant security controls in place. It seems, as always, convenience continues to be prioritised over security.

TruffleHog Update brings new AWS features

TruffleHog is an invaluable piece of software and has long been a part of my bag of tooling, so it was with some excitement when I read they had released a new component, AWS Analyze that can give greater insight into the key that has been exposed in your codebase.

"AWS Analyze resolves the caller's account and IAM principal type. For IAM users, it also retrieves groups and tags when available." - now, that is super useful. "AWS Analyze collects managed and inline policies associated with IAM users, groups, and roles, then presents their statements for review. Instead of gathering policies across IAM by hand, responders get the relevant identity and policy context in one focused view."

AWS Key exposure still remains a major issue - it's something all development teams can fall foul of with a slip of git ignore file configs or settings file additions in the middle of large commit changes. TruffleHog ran a verification on some findings and concluded : "Research on 64,024 leaked AWS keys found 88% were still active with a median age of five years, 84% had full administrator access, and 1 in 6 was a root key."

Still, in 2026, we find tht root keys are in use, exposed and not protected by companies or AWS. Providing additional insights into the owner of the key and it's status will help security teams trace issues faster. On the flip-side, any tool is a weapon in the right hands, so this is also a call for teams to make sure to double-up on protection of keys!

Manchester Airport Data Loss

Manchester Airports Group, operator of three of the UK's busiest airports, has revealed "a quantity of data" was stolen by a cyber extortion group during a recent incident that sees around 8.7 Million customers affected.

MAG believes the vast majority of those affected notes only the email addresses of customers were collected during the sign-up phase for the airport's public WiFi services.

In a statement, MAG disclosed "We have informed and are working with the relevant authorities; At no point has passenger safety or aviation security been compromised."

No airports in the group suffered any operational loss, however MAG temporarily revoked access to it's managed booking service "as a precautionary measure".

Anthropic Idiocy

Anthropic have announced in a somewhat off-hand way that their Claude AI models escaped their environment and compromised external companies earlier this year, even to the point that they ignored commands to cease activity. As with many other recent issues of AI Software going rogue, the company is trying to spin this as a "look how powerful this is!" way.

Hacker News has more detail on the event which continues to find me snapping expletives at my screen at the stupidity of the companies heralding their models with zero ability to show they have any control over them.

In what feels like a technological equivalent of the Radium mania of the early 20th Century, everyone has been trying to shove AI software into their products, touting it as the magical cure to our issues and senselessly pushing users to adopt it's usage. It's safe, they say, it's good for you, they say. What can we take from the recent announcements when we cut through the marketing hype?:

1. The companies producing their software have NO CONTROL over their software. This is concerning because they try to convince us that they have guardrails in place to protect our systems and others, yet their software appears to consistently ignore those guardrails and do the best it can to achieve a goal at all costs.
2. The companies producing their software take no responsibility for the illegal action their systems are taking, applauding the ability of their software, which is wildly out of control, for trespassing and compromising other systems.

This leads to a situation whereby we have to consider, seriously, are these companies actually keeping our data and infrastructure safe in our daily utilisation? If we had a wetware member of staff who ignored guidelines, refused to stop activity when ordered by a manager and illegally compromised other company networks and systems, would we celebrate them to the world or would we terminate their contract and call in the authorities?

The problems we've seen with AI Software going beyond their remit are programming issues that can be fixed. They are control boundaries that just need to be defined and locked-in, but the problem we face is that these companies just do not care. Unless we hold them to account, we're going to see more of these revelations and more of this marketing hype.

Just like the end of the Radium rush, will society be left saying "how could we have been so naive!?" in retrospect at the events of the last year and beyond?

Romanian Land Registry Hack

On Tuesday 14th July, the Romanian cadastre application, known as e-Terra, and internal email systems went offline. This very quickly led the Romania's real-estate market grinding to a halt as notaries could not authenticate sales, register mortgages, or it's citizens pull proof of ownership.

Romania's National Cyber Security Directorate (DNSC) has confirmed the attack was not a complex campaign and would have been prevented with some simple steps:
Known, unpatched vulnerabilities (that, according to the DNSC, had recently flagged to the Land Registry), combined with leaked credentials that were posted online, and a Group Policy named "DISABLE WINDOWS FIREWALL" led to the compromise. There was no zero-day, and the attacker did not need any form of bypass.

By Wednesday, stolen data was being offered for sale online in hacking forums and information surrounding the environment at the Land Registry was being offered-up.

ThreatLocker has a full write-up of the incident, highlighting how even simple, traditional methods can lead to disasterous implications without the need for APT-level technology to bring national services to a halt.

If ever there was a call to review your estate, policy configuration and vulnerability management programme, this is it.

Scattered Spider Convictions

The two teenagers arrested for the TfL cyber attack in June, Owen Flowers and Thalha Jubair, have been sentenced to five and a half years prison term.

The pair, part of the Scattered Spider group, had been known to police for years. Flowers had been given a cease-and-decist order for minor cyber crime in October 2023 as a 16-year old. Jubair had been arrested at the age of 14 in 2021. Jubair is also wanted in the US in connection with cyber crimes against 47 US-based victims - it's not clear as yet if he will be handed-over to US law enforcement upon completion of his prison term.

Woolwich Crown Court heard that whilst in prison awaiting trial, both teenagers were discovered with contraband phones continuing to discuss and coordinate future cyber attacks.

Police also seized cryptocurrency holdings worth around one million pounds in stolen or ransomed cryptocurrency.

Canada Secretly Cleans Botnet-Infected Devices

The Canadian Security Intelligence Service was given a warrant for threat reduction measures in a first-of-it's-kind ruling allowing them to "alter, degrade, and destroy botnet data on the infected machines and cut the devices loose from the networks". In other words, hack into devices, remove the crud and patch the infrastructure without the owner's intervention.

Involved in the Botnet were Canada-based servers, small office and home office routers, Internet-of-things devices, Ring doorbells, Security Cameras, TV's and Wifi-enabled devices.

Whilst the motive of preventing loss, theft of details, denial of service over services and further illegal activity, the modification of services and hardware with persistent fixes presents an ethical conundrum. Canada's court found the threat clearly established and imminent with the measures deemed necessary, reasonable and proportional. Do the end users and customers, however?

Reaching into a device that is not your own and wiping data is classified as computer mischief under the Criminal Code, hence the required court ruling before undertaking the work. It is not clear, however, how the agency collected information leading up to the request.

Should the customers have been notified first or in the process? Answers on a postcard...

Google security researcher denied earnings

Following on from Microsoft's recent spat, Google decided it wanted in on the action in denying researchers the finders fee they promised. In a recent bug report marked as high priority with a representative telling bug hunter, Justin O'Leary, "Nice Catch!", Google made a U-turn and decided that it wasn't an issue after all, but by design. Ah yes, that old chestnut eh?

Justin has published a nice blog article about the vulnerability which allows complete organisation takeover in Kubernetes namespaces with zero GCP permission.

After the initial praise in finding the bug, Google responded by saying "Cloud Vulnerability Reward Program panel has decided that the security impact of this issue does not meet the criteria to qualify for a reward.". Why so? you may ask? - "Rationale: We have reviewed your report and determined that the issue you described is working as intended. The Config Connector Service Account requires the organization admin role for the reported scenario to be exploitable which is not recommended and breaks best practice.".

There is but one small fly in the soup, however - and that being that Google actually demonstrates this practice in their help documents. Oooops!

So, how do we think security researchers with tasty zero-days are feeling right now? Pretty underappreciated and ready to drop hot potatoes all over the Internet is probably how you would expect. What the fallout of these big name brands reneging on their security research bounties will be over the coming months and years will be interesting to watch closely. We could either see responsible investigations and support hardening platforms dwindling, leading to epic unknown vulnerabilities, or a reduction in responsible disclosure and a higher quantity of public vulnerability information being thrown across the Internet.

One can only imagine the heated conversations between staff right now on both sides of this argument.

Microsoft spat with 0-day hunter continues

The ongoing spat between Microsoft and Nightmare Eclipse (aka Chaotic Eclipse) seems to be moving into damage control mode. With the feud spilling into security channels and warnings that major 0-day vulnerabilities will be disclosed in July, Microsoft now seems to be attempting to save face in the security research ecosystem.

With Nightmare-Eclipse booted from Github, kicked-out of legitimate channels and having payments withheld before being hung out and publicly accused of violating CVD, Microsoft finally seems to be reading the room as researchers consider their own future in disclosing major vulnerabilities with Microsoft products.

In a statement, Microsoft said it had "no intention to pursue action against individuals conducting or publishing security research", which is in stark contrast to threatening language days earlier in condemning public disclosure and warning it would work with law enforcement when criminal activity harmed customers.

Responsible disclosure with financial rewards is a valuable practice for businesses, but the vilification of those researchers following your processes and reneging on the deal will only lead to the possibility vulnerabilities will be sold to, shall we say, the more financially forthcoming and less ethically inclined channels.

We will have to see if there are further issues arising from the incident as the community looks on and watches unpatched vulnerabilities dropping into the public domain.

Checkmarx KICS compromised

Another day, another supply chain compromise, but this time within tooling used by vulnerability scanning. The Checkmarx KICS dockerhub repository was compromised with malicious images that overwrote existing tags. Uncovered by the team at Socket, the analysis shows the binary was modified to include data collection and exfiltration routines.

The evidence gathered showed uncensored scan reports were generated, encrypted and sent to an external address. This could potentially lead to the exfiltration of passwords or secrets and sensitive vulnerability information being utilised by attackers without the knowledge of the business utilising the scanning software.

KICS is utilised in a range of companies and in systems such as Datadog's IaC scanning software, leading to the potential for wider-scale compromise of businesses utilising their software. The new normal, it would appear, is that everywhere is being hit with supply chain attacks to work towards targets.

As always, keep an eye on the things that are watching things. I'm sure there's an old Soviet adage that could be employed here...

Axios Supply Chain Hack

The HTTP client, Axios, has been the victim of a supply chain attack after two new published versions (1.14.1 and 0.30.4) of the npm package included a malicious dependency, injecting plain-crypto-js.

The two versions were found to have been published using compromised npm credentials of the primary Axios maintainer, allowing the attackers to bypass the project's GitHub Actions CI/CD pipeline. Socket have a nice breakdown of the issue with details of the payload.

Users who have Axios versions 1.14.1 or 0.30.4 installed are required to immediately downgrade to a safe version and rotate credentials. The malicious versions have now been removed from npm.

Anyone who has installed the affected versions is also advised to remove plain-crypto-js form the node_modules directory and check for RAT artifacts. It is also advised to audit your CI/CD pipelines and block egress traffic to the command-and-control domain "sfrclak dot com".

Supply chain compromises will continue to be an ongoing issue as they work and fly under the radar in many cases. As always, lock your deployed software versions, ensure you have pipeline security scanning and maintain cool-off periods before deploying new versions anywhere near systems, never bleeding-edge production!

LiteLLM Compromised

Python Package Index have removed two versions of LiteLLM, the open source interface for accessing multiple large language models after a supply-chain attack injected them with credential-stealing code.

LiteLLM v1.82.7 and v1.82.8 have been removed due to malware in the litellm_init.pth file. The compromise appears to have originated form the use of Trivy in the project's CI/CD pipeline. According to details from Aqua Security, the attackers took advantage of a misconfiguration in Triby's GitHub Actions environment to steal a privileged access token allowing the manipulation of CI/CD, and the subsequent push of malicious Trivy versions ( v0.69.5 and v0.69.6 ) as DockerHub images.

The advisory warns anyone who has installed and run the project should assume any credentials available to the LiteLLM environment have been exposed and should be revoked or rotated immediately.

FCC Blocks Foreigners

In a country where Cisco kit used to be tampered with before export, the Government have now decided they don't want to potentially import foreign back doors into their marketplace through hardware and software.

The FCC have created a list of vendors that will now be unable to send new equipment to the US until they are verified and approved .

The manufacturer equipment is "deemed to pose an unacceptable risk to the national security of the United States or the security and safety of United States persons, based exclusively on any of four sources for such a determination and that such equipment or services possess certain capabilities as enumerated in section 2(a) of the Secure and Trusted Communications Networks Act of 2019", according to the ruling.

What it means in practice is that vendors are now selling the stock they have and citizens are free to utilise equipment they own, but new imports are blocked until they are cleared by the FCC.

The ruling determines "Any new router made outside the US will now need to be approved by the FCC before it can be imported, marketed, or sold in the country.", which is ironic as Cisco and Netgear build their equipment off-shore. Starlink is one of the only manufacturers to actually build equipment out of the 'States.

What will this mean in practice for organisations? Either higher costs for equipment, or utilisation of older, out-of-date equipment leading to potentially weaker security, most likely. Expect eBay prices for routers to go through the roof over the coming year.
